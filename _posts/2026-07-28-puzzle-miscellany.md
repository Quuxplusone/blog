I just finished reading Natan Last's 2025 book _Across the Universe: The Past, Present,
and Future of the Crossword Puzzle._ It was... fatiguing. Now, I myself am a "discursive"
writer, so I'm throwing stones from a glass house here; but it seemed to me that Last's
_every_ paragraph consisted of three or four anecdotes halfway told, capped off with some
nonsensical simile. Open the book at random to page 31:

> On December 11, 2022, Eric [Albert] suffered a hemorrhagic stroke of his cerebellum.
> He's lucky to be alive: around 50 percent of patients die in a month, half of those within
> the first two days. When we first spoke in April 2023, I immediately asked if his language
> had been affected, then regretted it. But he was fine, he said. The neurologists don't know
> why the stroke happened, or how he recovered his cognition so quickly. He says he repeated the
> French he was learning to himself in the ICU. I imagined him, was almost ashamed to imagine him,
> saying _ciré ciré ciré, épée épée épée_ — as if the accent marks were stray neurons in need of repair
> — or an item from James Joyce's _Finnegans Wake_ I'd just seen in _Word Ways_ because it's the
> only "word" with five or more Q's: _Quoiquoiquoiquoiquoiquoiquoiq!_ In Joyce's _Wake_, it's
> preceded by the line: "He lifts the lifewand and the dumb speak." I remembered analyses of
> Broca's aphasia I'd read studying neuroscience in college [...]

Page 163:

> In P.G. Wodehouse's 1957 novel, _Something Fishy_ (called _The Butler Did It_ in
> its American release), Lord Uffenham, every bit an imperious sneeze as his name suggests,
> barks at his wittier niece, Jane: "[...] Run and ask Keggs what the dickens 'Adventurer
> goes in for outrageous road-speed' is supposed to signify. Tell him it's urgent. And I want
> some more coffee... Yer can't drink too much coffee. It bucks you up. It stimulates the
> mental processes." The answer — DESPERADO, an "outrageous" anagram of ROAD SPEED and synonym
> of "adventurer" — is supplied by the august valet Augustus Keggs. Then Uffenham proceeds to
> a second stinker: "Desperado, eh? Capital. Now, go and ask him what the devil 'So the subordinate
> professional on trial gets wages in advance not without demur' means." For the intellectual or
> the upper-crust peer, the crossword and coffee must be consumed; who labors for it is immaterial.
> Eliot's brooder Prufrock "measured out [his] life in coffee spoons," Margaret Farrar called
> the _Times_ Saturday crossword a "two cups of coffee puzzle," Lord Uffenham has his niece
> make the coffee and his butler solve the puzzle.
>
> The butler did it; but who is there to
> say, besides Jane, that Uffenham didn't solve it himself? One imagines Keggs following
> Uffenham on a summery lawn the color of turtle shells, moving the lord's croquet balls,
> muttering "Very good shot, sir" as he does._ [...]

_Does_ one, though? Anyway, if you are excited by the mere idea of 300 pages of this sort of thing —
accent marks as damaged neurons, lawns the color of not just one turtle shell (i.e. a mottled
brown) but several, the names of Simon & Schuster inflated into "fat illegible balloons pricked
into oblivion by a skyscraper's spire" (page 50), a missed encounter between Vladimir Nabokov
and his future wife Véra described as "a word golf of only one, as if embalming a simpler past"
(page 169), etc., then you're in for a treat. I myself alternately slogged and skimmed.
Many of the book's chapters were first published individually in magazines, which explains
how the same anecdotes keep recurring.
"Three-letter word for a female swan" appears on page 54 and again on page 222.
There are two capsule biographies of Elizabeth Gorski and at least three of Arthur Wynne.

Last's shotgun approach does give starting points for some interesting web sleuthing.
For example, he reprints a butterfly-shaped Russian-language crossword drawn by Nabokov on 1926-07-06.
The image he uses just comes [from Wikipedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nabokov%27s_butterfly_crossword.jpg),
which got it from a tweet; but Nabokov's tiny handwriting is solvably transcribed
in [_Письма к Вере_](https://1.librebook.me/pisma_k_vere/vol4/1#:~:text=Crestos)
("Letters to Véra," 2014).
(Thanks to Russian StackExchange for that link!) The solution to Nabokov's butterfly
crossword is (spoiler) [here](/blog/code/2026-07-28-nabokov-butterfly-solution.md).

Last mentions that [Georges Perec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Perec),
of _La Disparition_ lipogram fame, also wrote crosswords (in French).
See one of Perec's, and solution, at
[Derek Harrison's blog](https://crosswordcentre.blogspot.com/p/georges-perec.html).
Perec's crosswords seem to be collected in two books:
[_Les mots croisés_](https://amzn.to/4vZi9Qx) (1979; ISBN 2863740032) and
[_Les mots croisés II_](https://amzn.to/4c7OAVI) (1986; ISBN 2867440661).

This reminds me that Stephen Sondheim, of Broadway fame, not only wrote cryptic
crosswords for _New York_ magazine in 1968–1969 but is thereby credited with
_introducing_ the cryptic to American audiences. His fantastic series of cryptics
is not hard to find online, e.g. [here](https://chall.us/misc_cryptics/nym_sondheim.pdf)
and [here](https://blogfott.blogspot.com/2014/07/putting-it-together.html) and
even on nymag.com ([1](https://nymag.com/docs/08/03/nymag-sondheim01.pdf),
[2](https://nymag.com/docs/08/03/nymag-sondheim02.pdf),
[3](https://nymag.com/docs/08/03/nymag-sondheim06.pdf)).
My favorite is 1968-04-29's "One Shy."

Fun fact: Sondheim apparently inspired the "Andrew Wyke" character in _Sleuth_.
(Run, don't walk, to see the [1972 film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleuth_(1972_film));
skip the bizarre 2007 remake.) From the _New York Times_ of
[1996-03-10](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/books/98/07/19/specials/sondheim-shaffer.html):

> SONDHEIM: Did I ever tell you about the time I met Sir Laurence Olivier?
> [He] said, "Oh, Mr. Sondheim, I'm so delighted to meet you. I've been playing you."
> Of course I thought he meant playing records ... I said, "Which show?" And he replied,
> "No, I've been playing you in the movie."

I should mention that a really _great_ book about crosswords and crosswording is
Coral Amende's [_The Crossword Obsession_](https://amzn.to/4x6FvEO) (2001).
It consists almost entirely of crossword constructors' own words,
and is fantastically entertaining.
[This anecdote](https://archive.org/details/crosswordobsessi00amen/page/n74) from John Samson
has lived rent-free in my head for the past 25 years:

> SAMSON: The first crossword I sent [NYT puzzle editor Eugene Maleska] was so bad
> he wrote back: "This is really amateurish. Do us both a favor and never send me
> another one again. One of your entries, OX MORGUE, clued as ‘Where dead oxen
> are carted off to,’ is ridiculous; also, your diagram is not symmetric, and on
> top of that it contains unkeyed letters." Needless to say, I noticed he had taken
> the time to reply to me (written in longhand), which prompted another effort on my part.
> ...
