> Antony stopped her lamentations and asked for a drink of wine, either because he was thirsty,
> or in the hope of a speedier release.  
> &emsp; &emsp; —Plutarch, _Life of Antony_ [77](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Antony*.html#77)
>
> ANTONY: I am dying, Egypt, dying.  
> Give me some Wine, and let me speak a little.  
> &emsp; &emsp; —Shakespeare, _Antony and Cleopatra_, V.i

The biographer of [William Haines Lytle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Haines_Lytle) writes,
of Lytle's poem ["Antony and Cleopatra"](https://archive.org/details/poemsofwilliamha00lytl/page/60/mode/2up):

> Bryant's "Library of Poetry and Song" is held responsible for
> having first put into circulation the absurd fiction that Lytle's
> famous poem was written while the author lay mortally wounded
> after the battle of Chickamauga.

In fact Lytle was killed at Chickamauga in September 1863, but his body received
"respectful and even reverential care [...] owing largely to the fact that the dead general
was recognized not only as a distinguished soldier, but also a poet." That night, writes
the body's Confederate warder, "in our bivouac by the camp-fire, we read the papers,
letters, and scraps of poetry that we found in the pocket-book" — but "Antony and Cleopatra"
wouldn't have been among them.

> The true history of the
> composition of the poem is this: "Antony and Cleopatra"
> was written at the Lytle homestead in the summer of 1858.
> The original manuscript was dashed off in a glow
> of creative excitement by the author, who left it lying upon
> a writing table in his private room. There it was found by
> Wm. W. Fosdick, [who] gave it to the editor of the _Cincinnati Commercial_ [...]
> The poem appeared in the _Commercial_ on July 29, 1858.

<table class="smaller">
<tr><td>I am dying, Egypt, dying!<br>
&emsp;Ebbs the crimson life-tide fast,<br>
And the dark Plutonian shadows<br>
&emsp;Gather on the evening blast;<br>
Let thine arms, O Queen, enfold me,<br>
&emsp;Hush thy sobs and bow thine ear;<br>
Listen to the great heart-secrets<br>
&emsp;Thou, and thou alone, must hear.<span class="pskip"></span><br>
Though my scarr’d and veteran legions<br>
&emsp;Bear their eagles high no more,<br>
And my wreck’d and scatter’d galleys<br>
&emsp;Strew dark Actium’s fatal shore,<br>
Though no glittering guards surround me,<br>
&emsp;Prompt to do their master’s will,<br>
I must perish like a Roman,<br>
&emsp;Die the great Triumvir still.<span class="pskip"></span><br>
[...]<span class="pskip"></span><br>
As for thee, star-eyed Egyptian,<br>
&emsp;Glorious sorceress of the Nile,<br>
Light the path to Stygian horrors<br>
&emsp;With the splendors of thy smile.<br>
Give the Cæsar crowns and arches,<br>
&emsp;Let his brow the laurel twine;<br>
I can scorn the Senate’s triumphs,<br>
&emsp;Triumphing in love like thine.<span class="pskip"></span><br>
I am dying, Egypt, dying;<br>
&emsp;Hark! th’ insulting foeman’s cry.<br>
They are coming! quick, my falchion,<br>
&emsp;Let me front them ere I die.<br>
Ah! no more amid the battle<br>
&emsp;Shall my heart exulting swell;<br>
Isis and Osiris guard thee!<br>
&emsp;Cleopatra, Rome, farewell!
</td></tr>
</table>

The biographer writes: "_Antony and Cleopatra_ is the author's masterpiece,
but not by any means his only good poem." True enough.

John Albro, in _Puck_ (1882-11-01),
[writes](https://archive.org/details/sim_puck_1882-11-01_12_295/page/n2), "Take Your Choice":

<table class="smaller">
<tr><td>I am dying, Egypt, dying,<br>
&emsp;Which I'm barely just alive<br>
From exhaustive efforts trying<br>
&emsp;To pronounce that word “<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Egyptian_War">Khedive</a>.”<span class="pskip"></span><br>
Are they lying, Egypt, lying,<br>
&emsp;Who assert we should receive<br>
"I" as "e"? And are they guying<br>
&emsp;When they call the cuss “Khedeve”?<span class="pskip"></span><br>
I am crying, Egypt, crying:<br>
&emsp;Orthographical contriver,<br>
In the face of English flying,<br>
&emsp;Won't it do to say “Khediver”?<span class="pskip"></span><br>
I am sighing, Egypt, sighing,<br>
&emsp;For my girl—can I believe her<br>
When she's twisting, when she's tying<br>
&emsp;Her sweet tongue into “Khedever”?<span class="pskip"></span><br>
I am nighing, Egypt, nighing<br>
&emsp;My <i>finale</i>. Ere ’tis over—<br>
Ere I’m frying, Egypt, frying—<br>
&emsp;I would fain suggest “Khedover.”
</td></tr>
</table>

And the Chicago _Tribune_ of 1889-06-02
[has](https://archive.org/details/per_chicago-daily-tribune_the-chicago-tribune_1889-06-02_49/page/n30/mode/1up):

<table class="smaller">
<tr><td>I am lying, Egypt, lying, in my own peculiar way,<br>
I acquired the habit lately, but I do it every day;<br>
Every morning to the river with my tackle I repair<br>
To beguile the speckled troutlet from its cool, pellucid lair.<br>
In the evening, on returning, I describe my victim's size,<br>
And I'm roaming, Egypt, roaming in a wilderness of lies.
</td></tr>
</table>

The "speckled troutlet" is perhaps borrowed
from Louisa J. Roberts' ["The Angler"](https://archive.org/details/biographicalske00robegoog/page/n273):
"To such cool shade, such limpid brook, / The angler comes with line and hook, / To tempt with his
enticing fly / The speckled troutlet whisking by."

I got onto this track after finding [a somewhat bit-rotted version](https://www.nyshistoricnewspapers.org/?a=d&d=tew19080728-01.1.11)
of that last poem in the New York _World_ of 1908-07-28.
The same day's _World_, [page 12](https://www.nyshistoricnewspapers.org/?a=d&d=tew19080728-01.1.12),
reports that heavyweight [Gus Ruhlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gus_Ruhlin) put up $500 bail each
for several men rounded up at an illicit boxing match, including one
"[Reuben Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rube_Goldberg), cartoonist." According to
[The Comics Journal](https://www.tcj.com/a-konversation-with-george-herrimans-biographer-michael-tisserand-part-two/),
Goldberg had been acting as timekeeper.

I was leafing through the _World_ because Robert Anton Wilson
[had written](https://archive.org/details/cosmic-trigger-vol.-1/page/92)
(in _Cosmic Trigger Vol. I_, 1977) that Charles Fort
[had written](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-ADE-388/page/863)
(in _Wild Talents_, 1932) that
the 1908-07-29 _World_ contained a story about a large black dog
walking up to two police detectives in Pittsburgh, emitting a hearty
"Good morning," and vanishing in a puff of greenish vapor.
In fact it does not. It can be nothing but coincidence that Wilson
misquotes Fort's source as "the _New York World_, July 2<b>7</b>, 1908,"
which _does_ contain something of the sort, on page 8, under the headline
"The Wonderful Talking Dog." Paraphrased:

> A penniless ventriloquist and his dog walk into a restaurant.
> "What'll you have?" says the proprietor. The ventriloquist cocks his head
> at the dog. "I'll have a ham sandwich," it says. The proprietor is amazed at
> the talking dog, and offers to buy it for $50 above and beyond the cost of the
> meal. Of course the ventriloquist accepts. As he's leaving, full and flush,
> he glances back at the dog he left behind: "You bum," it yells, "to sell me for $50!
> I'll show you — I'll never speak another word!" ...And it never did.
